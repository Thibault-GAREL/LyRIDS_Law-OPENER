Legal text is hard for named entity recognition. New statutes, parties and courts appear continually, and expert annotation is costly. Open-world NER can assign types that were not fixed at training time. These systems are built on general-domain text, though, and legal uses adapt them corpus by corpus and language by language. \rev{We take the opposite route, and apply OPENER unchanged}, a frugal pipeline of ready-to-use components. Its entity embedder stays generic, and no part of it is ever fitted to a legal schema. We test its two typing heads on four legal corpora, in English, Portuguese and German. One needs no target label at all, the other fits a small linear probe. We compare them against GLiNER, GNER, OWNER and a 4-bit Qwen, on quality, latency and energy at once. The transfer holds in English, and weakens across languages. The label-free head degrades sharply. The supervised probe is barely affected, and scores best overall (35.9 AMI end-to-end, 61.4 on gold mentions). Both stay one to two orders of magnitude cheaper than the generative baselines. \rev{Detection, not typing, is what caps every system here. The English detectors recover as little as 10.2\% of the German mentions. On the ones they do find, our supervised head assigns the right type 78.7\% of the time on average over the four corpora, against 64.6\% for the detector alone. Yet typing all of them correctly would still stop at 70 AMI out of 100.} Where labels exist, the supervised head is the practical operating point, and in-language detection is where adaptation should go first.
