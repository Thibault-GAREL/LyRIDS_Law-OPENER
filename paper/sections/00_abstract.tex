Legal text is hard for named entity recognition. New statutes, parties and courts appear continually, and expert annotation is costly. Open-world NER assigns types not fixed at training time, but these systems are built on general-domain text and adapted per corpus and language for legal use. \rev{We built OPENER for this setting instead, a frugal pipeline of ready-to-use components whose entity embedder is deliberately left generic, so that its space is never fitted to one legal schema and one legal corpus.} We evaluate its zero-shot head (OPENER-ZS) and supervised linear probe (OPENER-Sup) on four legal corpora in English, Portuguese and German, against GLiNER, GNER, OWNER and a 4-bit Qwen, reporting quality (Adjusted Mutual Information), latency and energy jointly over three seeds. \rev{The design holds under an English domain shift and degrades cross-lingually.} OPENER-ZS stays usable in English (up to 49.1 gold AMI) but \rev{does not lead the zero-shot field}, weakening cross-lingually (23.8--37.4), whereas OPENER-Sup is barely affected (61.4 on average, \rev{on a par with the 62.3 it reaches in a preliminary out-of-domain check}) and posts the best end-to-end score (35.9). Every system is then capped by the English detectors' recall (as low as 10.2\% on German), a ceiling both OPENER heads reach at one to two orders of magnitude less latency and energy than the generative baselines. With labels, the supervised head is the practical operating point, and detection, not typing, is where adaptation should go first. \rev{That a pipeline designed under legal constraints holds up this well is what motivates the general-domain study we are now running.}

% Named Entity Recognition (NER), a foundational Natural Language Processing task, extracts entities (persons, organisations, dates) from raw text. However, real-world applications rarely afford a static class inventory, and the legal domain is an extreme case: new statutes, parties, instruments, courts and jurisdictions appear continually, expert annotation is expensive, and errors carry material consequences. Open-world NER solves this by assigning types not fixed at training time, but the systems that do so are built and tuned on general-domain text. Their legal uses adapt them per corpus, schema and language, by fine-tuning a tagger or by prompting a large model anew. How far such a pipeline transfers to legal text when it is reused unchanged, across languages, is left largely unmeasured.

% OPENER is a frugal open-world NER pipeline, accurate on general-domain text and assembled entirely from off-the-shelf pretrained components. We ask whether it transfers to legal text, with no legal-domain retraining of any component. We reuse its contrastively fine-tuned, Matryoshka-truncatable entity embedder unchanged behind a frozen open-world detector, and benchmark its two typing heads, a label-free zero-shot head (OPENER-ZS) and a supervised linear probe (OPENER-Sup), on four legal NER corpora spanning three languages: English (SEC filings and Indian court judgements), Portuguese (Brazilian court decisions) and German (federal court decisions). Following the general-domain protocol, we report quality (Adjusted Mutual Information), latency and energy consumption jointly, with every OPENER score averaged over three embedder-retraining seeds.

% Transfer holds under a pure English domain shift and degrades once a cross-lingual shift is added. OPENER-ZS stays competitive on English legal text (up to 49.1 gold AMI), but it no longer leads the zero-shot field as it did in the general domain. It falls behind the GLiNER family and, on average, behind OWNER, and it weakens sharply cross-lingually (23.8--37.4 gold AMI). OPENER-Sup is barely affected by the shift (61.4 mean gold AMI, against 62.3 in the general domain) and posts the best end-to-end AMI throughout (35.9). Its balanced linear probe separates closely related legal types, such as lawyers from judges or statutes from case law, where the shared embedding space places their vectors close together. Label-name matching cannot draw that boundary without target supervision. End-to-end, every system falls into a narrow band capped by the English detector's recall (down to 18.7\% on German), at one to two orders of magnitude less latency and energy than the generative baselines. Where labelled examples are available, the supervised head is therefore the more practical operating point, and in-language detection, not typing, is where further adaptation would pay off first.
