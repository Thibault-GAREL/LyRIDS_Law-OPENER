\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:exp:datasets}
We evaluate on four legal-specific NER sets across three languages that, together, separate two kinds of distribution shift. \emph{Two are in English}, isolating a \emph{pure domain shift} from the general-domain spans the embedder was fine-tuned on: E-NER \citep{au2022ener} (annotated U.S.\ SEC/EDGAR filings, $7$ types) and Indian-Legal \citep{kalamkar2022indianlegal} (Indian court judgements, $14$ fine-grained legal types). LeNER-Br \citep{luzdearaujo2018lenerbr} (Brazilian court decisions, Portuguese) and German-LER \citep{leitner2020germanler} (German federal court decisions) add a \emph{cross-lingual} shift. All four annotate \emph{legal-specific} entity types (courts, statutes, legislation, case law, provisions), rather than the generic person/organisation/date schema of general NER. \autoref{tab:legal-datasets} summarises them with the entity-type distribution of their test mentions.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp:setup}
Embedder, detector and heads are exactly those of \autoref{sec:method}, and nothing is retrained on legal data: Nomic v1.5 at $D{=}768$ in span-in-context mode, reused as released, behind a frozen GLiNER detector at threshold $0.3$ (GLiNER-L for OPENER-ZS, GLiNER-M for OPENER-Sup, whose probe is fit on its own train detections). The baselines are GLiNER-S/M/L, GNER-T5-base, OWNER and a 4-bit Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, all prompted zero-shot with the target type names. Test splits are capped at the first $1000$ entity-bearing sentences, with Qwen on a $200$-sentence subsample.

\autoref{tab:legal-setup} in \autoref{sec:appendix:setup} gives the complete configuration, including the typing hyperparameters, all fixed a priori and never tuned on a legal set: an English anchor dictionary of one to five words per type, embedded in four short templates before averaging, three transductive $k$-means iterations, $\beta{=}0.05$, and $C{=}1$ for the balanced linear probe. Evaluation code, anchor dictionaries and per-run logs are released anonymously with the submission.

The two protocols do not exercise the same zero-shot head. End-to-end, OPENER-ZS is the full head of \autoref{sec:method}, templated prototypes, transductive refinement and detector fusion. Typing on gold there is no detector in the loop, hence no label to fuse, so the head reduces to the plain nearest-prototype classifier: \autoref{tab:legal-gold} measures the label-name geometry on its own, \autoref{tab:legal-main} the operating point one would deploy.

We report two orthogonal sources of uncertainty. The embedder's \emph{training} is stochastic, so the whole study is repeated under three retraining seeds, every OPENER cell giving mean\,$\pm$\,std, the baselines being frozen deterministic checkpoints run once. Inference is deterministic for a fixed embedder, so the remaining uncertainty is the finite test set, quantified with a paired bootstrap in \autoref{sec:exp:results}. Efficiency is deliberately \emph{not} seed-averaged: a seed changes the embedder's weights, not its per-sentence FLOPs, so any spread would reflect the machine's thermal state.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:exp:results}
Keeping the two protocols apart, \autoref{tab:legal-gold} reports typing-on-gold AMI and \autoref{tab:legal-main} the end-to-end comparison on all three axes. OWNER appears in both, as the one baseline that types mentions it is given rather than detecting and typing jointly. Three findings stand out.

\textbf{Label-free typing is strong in English but degrades cross-lingually.} OPENER-ZS is usable on the two English sets ($47.7$ on E-NER and $49.1$ on Indian, from English label-name prototypes alone), but it drops to $37.4$ (pt) and $23.8$ (de). Its English prototypes align well with English legal mentions, far less with other-language ones (\autoref{tab:legal-gold}). It also trails OWNER's own label-free typing on all four sets ($44.7$ mean against $39.5$), which trains an entity encoder rather than reusing one, so the label-free operating point is where reuse costs the most. The zero-shot head is where the cross-lingual distribution shift bites hardest. It is also the \emph{seed-sensitive} head: its gold scores vary by ${\pm}3$--$6$ AMI across the three embedder retrainings (the released one is in fact a low draw on the English sets), whereas the supervised probe varies by at most ${\pm}1.4$, because label-name prototypes depend on the fine geometry of the embedding space, which shifts from one contrastive retraining to the next, while a fitted probe absorbs those shifts.

\textbf{Supervised typing transfers well, including across languages, because the separation it learns is geometric, not just statistical.} Fitting a balanced linear probe on the same frozen, general-domain embedder reaches $61.4$ mean typing-on-gold AMI over the four sets ($70.9$ and $60.8$ on the two English corpora, and still $53.7$ and $60.3$ on Portuguese LeNER-Br and German-LER), and is stable across the embedder-retraining seeds (std ${\leq}1.4$ AMI). It leads OWNER on every set, by $16$ AMI on E-NER and by $35$ on German. It scores $62.3$ in a preliminary general-domain check (ongoing work), so keeping the space unspecialised costs under a point of AMI here: a balanced probe fits hyperplanes precise enough to pull apart legal types whose vectors sit \emph{close together} (a lawyer from a judge, a statute from a case citation), which a single label-name match cannot do without supervision. The probe adapts to whatever the embedder encodes, so target supervision largely absorbs the cross-lingual gap at typing time.

\textbf{End-to-end, detection sets the ceiling, and the label-free head cannot pull ahead of the detector it fuses with.} On these legal-specific schemas every system clusters in a narrow $\approx 33$--$36$ AMI band (\autoref{tab:legal-main}), because the English detectors recover only a fraction of gold mentions (GLiNER-L offset recall $18.7$--$57.4\%$, GLiNER-M $10.2$--$61.1\%$). With so few mentions detected, no typing head can pull far ahead, and OPENER-ZS's fusion essentially reverts to the GLiNER-L labels. Per-dataset winners are specific (OWNER \citep{genest2025owner} on E-NER, GNER-T5 on German), though OWNER degrades most cross-lingually at several times the inference cost of either OPENER head, and Qwen trails throughout while being the most energy-hungry system per sentence. \emph{Averaged over the four sets, OPENER-Sup posts the best end-to-end AMI ($35.9$)}, narrowly ahead of GLiNER-L ($35.0$) and GLiNER-M ($34.9$), for every retraining seed. OPENER-ZS ($33.4$) sits just below OWNER ($33.7$), well under the $39.5$ the same fused head reaches in our preliminary general-domain runs, where it leads GLiNER-L's $38.9$ (out-of-domain end-to-end figures, unrelated to the legal gold mean of \autoref{tab:legal-gold}). The margins are small, as expected when detection is the ceiling, yet a \emph{paired} bootstrap on the released model ($K{=}1000$) makes OPENER-Sup's lead \textbf{statistically significant} ($+1.0$ AMI over both GLiNER-M and GLiNER-L, $95\%$ CI $[+0.4,+1.7]$ and $[+0.1,+1.9]$), while OPENER-ZS's fusion sits significantly \emph{below} GLiNER-L ($-1.3$, $[-1.8,-0.8]$), largely deferring to the detector's labels. The typing head is therefore not the weak link, consistent with the typing-on-gold results.

\subsection{Analysis}
\label{sec:exp:analysis}
Two distribution shifts act together here, and the results separate them cleanly. Under a pure \emph{domain} shift (E-NER), the deliberately unspecialised embedder already places legal types far enough apart to be separated, by a label-name prototype ($47.7$ gold AMI, no target label at all) or, far more precisely, by a supervised hyperplane ($70.9$). A cross-lingual shift widens the gap, the label-free head dropping to $24$--$37$ while the probe stays high ($53.7$/$60.3$) by re-fitting on target labels.

A specialised-schema stress test isolates fineness of separation from language. On German-LER's fine $19$-type schema OPENER-ZS falls further to $22.7_{\pm2.1}$ gold AMI, as fine German type codes rarely have a faithful English label-name prototype, while OPENER-Sup still reaches $61.5_{\pm0.7}$, \emph{on a par with its coarse score} ($60.3$) despite nearly three times as many classes. The embedder separates even fine legal distinctions once a probe names them, so the zero-shot weakness is not the embedding geometry but the missing supervised signal.

The binding constraint end-to-end is detection, not typing: GLiNER-L's offset recall falls from $57.4\%$ (en) to $18.7\%$ (de), and GLiNER-M's, which OPENER-Sup runs on, from $61.1\%$ to $10.2\%$, so the scores compress to a ${\approx}33$--$36$ band whatever the typing head, within which OPENER-Sup still posts the best average ($35.9$) by re-typing the detected mentions with supervision.

Language alone does not explain that fall: GLiNER-L recovers $43.9\%$ of the Portuguese mentions, on a par with the $44.5\%$ it reaches on English Indian judgements, and only German collapses. Its schema is as singular as its language, since legal-norm and case-law citations make up $62\%$ of its mentions and are long multi-token strings, while our alignment credits a mention only on an exact match of both character offsets. Cross-lingual transfer and long-span detection are therefore confounded there, and separating them takes a boundary-relaxed alignment we leave to future work. The reading is the same either way: an in-language \emph{detector} would lift the ceiling far more than a better typing head. Within our frugal, retraining-free budget, OPENER-Sup is therefore the practical default wherever even a modest labelled sample exists, and OPENER-ZS a no-label fallback for target languages close to the embedder's own.

\textbf{Fusion weight $\beta$.} The weight $\beta$ balances the label-name centroids against the detector's own labels. It is fixed a priori at $0.05$, and sweeping it post-hoc on the released model, a pure inference knob that leaves the embedder untouched, tells the same story: at $\beta{=}0$ the refined prototypes alone reach $32.4$ average AMI ($30.5$ without the transductive pass), and as $\beta$ grows the head interpolates up to the GLiNER-L labels ($35.0$), a plateau reached by $\beta{\approx}0.2$. The label-free head can therefore do no better end-to-end than the detector it fuses with. The sweep also exposes a component that does not survive the transfer: at $\beta{=}0.05$, dropping the transductive pass would \emph{raise} the average from $33.7$ to $34.8$ (released seed, hence the offset from the $33.4$ of \autoref{tab:legal-main}). Re-estimating prototypes on test mentions helps when those are mostly true entities, as they are in our general-domain runs, whereas here two thirds of the detected spans are alignment noise and the centroids drift towards it. We keep the released configuration rather than tune it on legal test sets, but under a domain and language shift the label-free components are the ones to re-examine first.
