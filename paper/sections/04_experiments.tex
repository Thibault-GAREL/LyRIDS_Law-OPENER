\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:exp:datasets}
We evaluate on four legal-specific NER sets across three languages that, together, separate two kinds of distribution shift. \emph{Two are in English}, isolating a \emph{pure domain shift} from the general-domain spans the embedder was fine-tuned on: E-NER \citep{au2022ener} (annotated U.S.\ SEC/EDGAR filings, $7$ types) and Indian-Legal \citep{kalamkar2022indianlegal} (Indian court judgements, $14$ fine-grained legal types). LeNER-Br \citep{luzdearaujo2018lenerbr} (Brazilian court decisions, Portuguese) and German-LER \citep{leitner2020germanler} (German federal court decisions) add a \emph{cross-lingual} shift. All four annotate \emph{legal-specific} entity types (courts, statutes, legislation, case law, provisions), rather than the generic person/organisation/date schema of general NER. \autoref{tab:legal-datasets} summarises them with the entity-type distribution of their test mentions.

\rev{\autoref{tab:legal-datasets} also quotes the supervised topline published with each corpus ($90.5$ to $96.0$ entity-level F1), which sets the scale our end-to-end numbers should be read against. \autoref{sec:exp:analysis} shows how much of the gap to those figures is detection rather than typing.}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp:setup}
Embedder, detector and heads are exactly those of \autoref{sec:method}, and nothing is retrained on legal data: Nomic v1.5 at $D{=}768$ in span-in-context mode, reused as released, behind a frozen GLiNER detector at threshold $0.3$ (GLiNER-L for OPENER-ZS, GLiNER-M for OPENER-Sup, whose probe is fit on its own train detections). The baselines are GLiNER-S/M/L, GNER-T5-base, OWNER and \rev{an instruction-tuned LLM baseline, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct in $4$-bit}, all prompted zero-shot with the target type names. Test splits are capped at the first $1000$ entity-bearing sentences, with Qwen on a $200$-sentence subsample. \rev{That model is the largest our $6$\,GB GPU runs, and a hosted frontier model would have made the latency and energy axes unmeasurable, a constraint we spell out in the Limitations.}

\autoref{tab:legal-setup} in \autoref{sec:appendix:setup} gives the complete configuration. Every typing hyperparameter of \autoref{sec:method} is fixed a priori and never tuned on a legal set, $C{=}1$ for the balanced probe included. \rev{Evaluation code, anchor dictionaries, dataset loaders and per-run logs are public.\footnote{\scriptsize\url{https://github.com/Thibault-GAREL/LyRIDS_Law-OPENER}}}

The two protocols do not exercise the same zero-shot head (\autoref{tab:protocols}): \autoref{tab:legal-gold} measures the label-name geometry on its own, \autoref{tab:legal-main} the operating point one would deploy.

We report two orthogonal sources of uncertainty. The embedder's \emph{training} is stochastic, so the whole study is repeated under three retraining seeds, every OPENER cell giving mean\,$\pm$\,std, the baselines being frozen deterministic checkpoints run once. Inference is deterministic for a fixed embedder, so the remaining uncertainty is the finite test set, quantified with a paired bootstrap in \autoref{sec:exp:results}. Efficiency is deliberately \emph{not} seed-averaged: a seed changes the embedder's weights, not its per-sentence FLOPs, so any spread would reflect the machine's thermal state.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:exp:results}
Keeping the two protocols apart, \autoref{tab:legal-gold} reports typing-on-gold AMI and \autoref{tab:legal-main} the end-to-end comparison on all three axes. OWNER appears in both, as the one baseline that types mentions it is given rather than detecting and typing jointly. Three findings stand out.

\textbf{Label-free typing is strong in English but degrades cross-lingually.} OPENER-ZS is usable on the two English sets ($47.7$ on E-NER and $49.1$ on Indian, from English label-name prototypes alone), but it drops to $37.4$ (pt) and $23.8$ (de). Its English prototypes align well with English legal mentions, far less with other-language ones (\autoref{tab:legal-gold}). It also trails OWNER's own label-free typing on all four sets ($44.7$ mean against $39.5$), which trains an entity encoder rather than reusing one, so the label-free operating point is where reuse costs the most. The zero-shot head is where the cross-lingual distribution shift bites hardest. It is also the \emph{seed-sensitive} head: its gold scores vary by ${\pm}3$--$6$ AMI across the three embedder retrainings (the released one is in fact a low draw on the English sets), whereas the supervised probe varies by at most ${\pm}1.4$, because label-name prototypes depend on the fine geometry of the embedding space, which shifts from one contrastive retraining to the next, while a fitted probe absorbs those shifts.

\textbf{Supervised typing transfers well, including across languages, because the separation it learns is geometric, not just statistical.} Fitting a balanced linear probe on the same frozen, general-domain embedder reaches $61.4$ mean typing-on-gold AMI over the four sets ($70.9$ and $60.8$ on the two English corpora, and still $53.7$ and $60.3$ on Portuguese LeNER-Br and German-LER), and is stable across the embedder-retraining seeds (std ${\leq}1.4$ AMI). It leads OWNER on every set, by $16$ AMI on E-NER and by $35$ on German. It scores $62.3$ in a preliminary general-domain check (ongoing work), so keeping the space unspecialised costs under a point of AMI here: a balanced probe fits hyperplanes precise enough to pull apart legal types whose vectors sit \emph{close together} (a lawyer from a judge, a statute from a case citation), which a single label-name match cannot do without supervision. The probe adapts to whatever the embedder encodes, so target supervision largely absorbs the cross-lingual gap at typing time. \rev{Macro-F1 over the same gold predictions tells the same story ($89.1$ on E-NER, $74.6$ on average, against $46.1$ for the label-free head), so none of these conclusions hinges on the choice of metric. Those figures type mentions that are given, whereas the published toplines of \autoref{sec:exp:datasets} include detection, so the two are not two sides of one comparison. What the pair does show is that typing is not where a frozen, general-domain space falls short.}

\textbf{End-to-end, detection sets the ceiling, and the label-free head cannot pull ahead of the detector it fuses with.} On these legal-specific schemas every system clusters in a narrow $\approx 33$--$36$ AMI band (\autoref{tab:legal-main}), because the English detectors recover only a fraction of gold mentions (GLiNER-L offset recall $18.7$--$57.4\%$, GLiNER-M $10.2$--$61.1\%$). With so few mentions detected, no typing head can pull far ahead. Per-dataset winners are specific (OWNER \citep{genest2025owner} on E-NER, GNER-T5 on German), though OWNER degrades most cross-lingually at several times the cost of either OPENER head, and Qwen trails throughout while being the most energy-hungry per sentence. \emph{Averaged over the four sets, OPENER-Sup posts the best end-to-end AMI ($35.9$)}, narrowly ahead of GLiNER-L ($35.0$) and GLiNER-M ($34.9$), for every retraining seed, and OPENER-ZS ($33.4$) sits just below OWNER ($33.7$). The margins are small, as expected when detection is the ceiling, yet a \emph{paired} bootstrap on the released model ($K{=}1000$) makes OPENER-Sup's lead \textbf{statistically significant} ($+1.0$ AMI over both GLiNER-M and GLiNER-L, $95\%$ CI $[+0.4,+1.7]$ and $[+0.1,+1.9]$), while OPENER-ZS's fusion sits significantly \emph{below} GLiNER-L ($-1.3$, $[-1.8,-0.8]$), largely deferring to the detector's labels.

\subsection{Analysis}
\label{sec:exp:analysis}
Two distribution shifts act together here, and the results separate them. Under a pure \emph{domain} shift (E-NER), the deliberately unspecialised embedder already places legal types far enough apart for a label-name prototype to separate them ($47.7$ gold AMI, no target label at all), and for a supervised hyperplane to do so far more precisely ($70.9$). A cross-lingual shift widens that gap, the label-free head dropping to $24$--$37$ while the probe stays high ($53.7$/$60.3$).

A specialised-schema stress test isolates fineness of separation from language. On German-LER's fine $19$-type schema OPENER-ZS falls further to $22.7_{\pm2.1}$ gold AMI, as fine German type codes rarely have a faithful English label-name prototype, while OPENER-Sup still reaches $61.5_{\pm0.7}$, \emph{on a par with its coarse score} ($60.3$) despite nearly three times as many classes. The embedder separates even fine legal distinctions once a probe names them, so the zero-shot weakness is not the embedding geometry but the missing supervised signal.

Language alone does not explain the collapse of detection on German: GLiNER-L recovers $43.9\%$ of the Portuguese mentions, on a par with the $44.5\%$ it reaches on English Indian judgements, and only German collapses. Its schema is as singular as its language, since legal-norm and case-law citations make up $62\%$ of its mentions and are long multi-token strings, while our alignment credits a mention only on an exact match of both character offsets. The reading is the same either way: an in-language \emph{detector} would lift the ceiling far more than a better typing head. Within our frugal, retraining-free budget, OPENER-Sup is therefore the practical default wherever even a modest labelled sample exists, and OPENER-ZS a no-label fallback for target languages close to the embedder's own.

\rev{\textbf{Where end-to-end quality is actually lost.} \autoref{tab:legal-decomp} splits each end-to-end score into its two stages, each OPENER head sharing its detector with the baseline above it so that the rows of a pair differ \emph{only} in the typing head. The supervised head is then worth far more than its $+1.0$ AMI suggests: on correctly detected mentions it assigns the right type $78.7\%$ of the time against the detector's own $64.6\%$, a $14$-point gain the average hides because it applies only to the $35.7\%$ of mentions that survive detection. The zero-shot head moves the other way ($64.5\%$ against GLiNER-L's $70.1\%$), which is why its fusion sits below the detector it fuses with. The oracle column settles the ceiling question: typing those same spans \emph{perfectly} would reach only ${\approx}42$ AMI, not $100$.}

\rev{\textbf{Most detection failures are boundaries, not blindness.} Counting a gold mention as \emph{touched} when a prediction overlaps it without necessarily matching both offsets, GLiNER-L touches $68.6\%$ of gold mentions on average but matches only $41.1\%$ exactly. On Indian-Legal the gap is widest ($44.5\%$ exact against $79.7\%$ touched), so the detector usually finds the mention and disagrees about where it ends, which long statute and precedent citations make routine. German is the one corpus this does not rescue: even relaxed, GLiNER-L reaches $38.4\%$ there. Cross-lingual transfer and long-span detection were confounded in that corpus, and this separates them: German fails as a language, not only as a schema of long spans.}

\rev{\textbf{What the two heads confuse.} The confusions are exactly the ones the design predicts (\autoref{sec:appendix:errors}). On German-LER, OPENER-ZS maps \textsc{norm} onto \textsc{regulation} $213$ times, by far its dominant error, since the two English anchor names are near-synonyms while the corpus separates them sharply. The probe makes that confusion $3$ times, and on citations such as \emph{\S\,63 Abs.\,1 AO} it answers \textsc{norm} where the zero-shot head answers \textsc{regulation}. On Indian-Legal, OPENER-ZS collapses \textsc{other-person} into the procedural roles \textsc{petitioner}, \textsc{witness} and \textsc{respondent} $123$ times, which no label-name prototype can avoid: the four types all name people, and which applies depends on a procedural role rather than on any surface form. This is the mechanism behind the gap between the two heads, and it is why supervision, not a better embedder, is what the label-free head lacks.}

\textbf{Fusion weight $\beta$.} \rev{Sweeping $\beta$ post-hoc (\autoref{sec:appendix:beta}) confirms the reading above: the label-free head interpolates from the refined prototypes alone ($32.4$ average AMI at $\beta{=}0$) up to the GLiNER-L labels ($35.0$), a plateau reached by $\beta{\approx}0.2$, so it can do no better end-to-end than the detector it fuses with. The sweep also shows the transductive pass failing to survive the transfer, which makes the label-free components the ones to re-examine first under a domain and language shift.}
