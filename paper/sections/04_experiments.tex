\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Datasets}
\label{sec:exp:datasets}
We evaluate on four legal-specific NER sets across three languages that, together, separate two kinds of distribution shift. \emph{Two are in English}, isolating a \emph{pure domain shift} from the general-domain data the embedder was trained on: E-NER \citep{au2022ener} (annotated U.S.\ SEC/EDGAR filings, $7$ types) and Indian-Legal \citep{kalamkar2022indianlegal} (Indian court judgments, $14$ fine-grained legal types). LeNER-Br \citep{luzdearaujo2018lenerbr} (Brazilian court decisions, Portuguese) and German-LER \citep{leitner2020germanler} (German federal court decisions) add a \emph{cross-lingual} shift. All four annotate \emph{legal-specific} entity types (courts, statutes, legislation, case law, provisions), rather than the generic person/organisation/date schema of general NER. \autoref{tab:legal-datasets} summarises them with the entity-type distribution of their test mentions.

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:exp:setup}
Embedder, detector and heads are exactly those of the general-domain OPENER (\autoref{sec:method}) and nothing is retrained on legal data: Nomic v1.5 at $D{=}768$ in span-in-context mode, reused as released, behind a frozen GLiNER detector at threshold $0.3$ (GLiNER-L for OPENER-ZS, GLiNER-M for OPENER-Sup, whose probe is fit on its own train detections). The baselines are GLiNER-S/M/L, GNER-T5-base, OWNER and a 4-bit Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct, all prompted zero-shot with the target type names, and all compared end-to-end only, since they detect and type jointly. Test splits are capped at the first $1000$ sentences, with Qwen on a $200$-sentence subsample. \autoref{tab:legal-setup} in \autoref{sec:appendix:setup} gives the complete configuration, including the typing hyperparameters, which are inherited unchanged from the general-domain study: an English anchor dictionary of two to five words per type, embedded in four short templates before averaging, three transductive $k$-means iterations, $\beta{=}0.05$, and $C{=}1$ for the balanced linear probe. Evaluation code, anchor dictionaries and per-run logs will be released.

The two protocols do not exercise the same zero-shot head. End-to-end, OPENER-ZS is the full head of \autoref{sec:method}, templated prototypes, transductive refinement and detector fusion. Typing on gold there is no detector in the loop, hence no label to fuse and no detection noise to absorb, so the head reduces to the plain nearest-prototype classifier: \autoref{tab:legal-gold} measures the label-name geometry on its own, \autoref{tab:legal-main} the operating point one would deploy.

Two further points deserve a word. First, we report two orthogonal sources of uncertainty. The reused embedder's \emph{training} is stochastic, so we repeat the whole legal study under the three retraining seeds and report mean\,$\pm$\,std in every OPENER cell, while the baselines are frozen, deterministic checkpoints reported as single runs. Inference itself is deterministic for a fixed embedder, so the remaining uncertainty is the finite test set, quantified with a paired bootstrap for the significance test in \autoref{sec:exp:results}. Second, efficiency is deliberately \emph{not} averaged over the retraining seeds. A different seed changes the embedder's weights but not its architecture or per-sentence FLOPs, so any spread across seed re-runs would reflect the machine's thermal state, not the model.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:exp:results}
Keeping the two protocols apart, \autoref{tab:legal-gold} reports typing-on-gold AMI (OPENER heads, perfect detection) and \autoref{tab:legal-main} the end-to-end comparison against the baselines, on all three axes at once. Three findings stand out.

\textbf{Label-free typing is strong in English but degrades cross-lingually.} OPENER-ZS is usable on the two English sets ($47.7$ on E-NER and $49.1$ on Indian, from English label-name prototypes alone), but it drops to $37.4$ (pt) and $23.8$ (de). Its English prototypes align well with English legal mentions, far less with other-language ones (\autoref{tab:legal-gold}). The zero-shot head is where the cross-lingual distribution shift bites hardest. It is also the \emph{seed-sensitive} head: its gold scores vary by ${\pm}3$--$6$ AMI across the three embedder retrainings (the released one is in fact a low draw on the English sets), whereas the supervised probe varies by at most ${\pm}1.4$, because label-name prototypes depend on the fine geometry of the embedding space, which shifts from one contrastive retraining to the next, while a fitted probe absorbs those shifts.

\textbf{Supervised typing transfers well, including across languages, because the separation it learns is geometric, not just statistical.} Fitting a balanced linear probe on the same frozen, general-domain embedder reaches $61.4$ mean typing-on-gold AMI over the four sets ($70.9$ and $60.8$ on the two English corpora, and still $53.7$ and $60.3$ on Portuguese LeNER-Br and German-LER), and is stable across the embedder-retraining seeds (std ${\leq}1.4$ AMI). This is barely below OPENER-Sup's own general-domain score ($62.3$), even though the embedder was never shown a single legal type: a balanced probe fits hyperplanes precise enough to pull apart legal types whose vectors sit \emph{close together} (a lawyer from a judge, a statute from a case citation), which a single label-name match cannot do without supervision. The probe adapts to whatever the embedder encodes, so target supervision largely absorbs the cross-lingual gap at typing time.

\textbf{End-to-end, detection sets the ceiling, and OPENER-ZS's standing slips relative to the general domain.} On these legal-specific schemas every system clusters in a narrow $\approx 33$--$36$ AMI band (\autoref{tab:legal-main}), because the English detectors recover only a fraction of gold mentions (GLiNER-L offset recall $18.7$--$57.4\%$). With so few mentions detected, no typing head can pull far ahead, and OPENER-ZS's fusion essentially reverts to the GLiNER-L labels. Per-dataset winners are specific (OWNER \citep{genest2025owner} on E-NER, GNER-T5 on German), though OWNER degrades most cross-lingually at an order-of-magnitude higher inference cost, and Qwen trails throughout while being the slowest. \emph{Averaged over the four sets, OPENER-Sup posts the best end-to-end AMI ($35.9$)}, narrowly ahead of GLiNER-L ($35.0$) and GLiNER-M ($34.9$), for every retraining seed. OPENER-ZS ($33.4$) sits just below OWNER ($33.7$), a marked step down from its \emph{general-domain} standing, where the same fused zero-shot head led every compared system end-to-end ($39.5$ vs GLiNER-L's $38.9$). The margins are small, as expected when detection is the ceiling, yet a \emph{paired} bootstrap on the released model ($K{=}1000$) makes OPENER-Sup's lead \textbf{statistically significant} ($+1.0$ AMI over both GLiNER-M and GLiNER-L, $95\%$ CI $[+0.4,+1.7]$ and $[+0.1,+1.9]$), while OPENER-ZS's fusion sits significantly \emph{below} GLiNER-L ($-1.3$, $[-1.8,-0.8]$), largely deferring to the detector's labels. The typing head is therefore not the weak link, consistent with the typing-on-gold results.

\subsection{Analysis}
\label{sec:exp:analysis}
Two distribution shifts act together here, and the results separate them cleanly. Under a pure \emph{domain} shift (E-NER), the general-domain embedder already places legal types far enough apart to be separated, whether by a label-name prototype ($47.7$ gold AMI, no target label at all) or, far more precisely, by a supervised hyperplane ($70.9$). Adding a cross-lingual shift widens the gap, the label-free head dropping to $24$--$37$ while the probe stays high ($53.7$/$60.3$) because it re-fits on target labels.

The specialised-schema stress test isolates fineness of separation from language. On German-LER's fine $19$-type schema OPENER-ZS falls further to $22.7_{\pm2.1}$ gold AMI, as fine German type codes often have no faithful English label-name prototype, while OPENER-Sup still reaches $61.5_{\pm0.7}$, \emph{on a par with its $7$-type coarse score} ($60.3$) despite nearly three times as many classes. The embedder separates even fine legal distinctions once a probe names them, so the zero-shot weakness is not the embedding geometry but the missing supervised signal to exploit it.

The binding constraint end-to-end is detection, not typing: GLiNER-L's offset recall falls from $57.4\%$ (en) to $18.7\%$ (de), so the end-to-end scores compress to a ${\approx}33$--$36$ band regardless of the typing head, within which OPENER-Sup nonetheless posts the best average ($35.9$) by re-typing the detected mentions with target-label supervision.

Language alone does not explain that fall: GLiNER-L recovers $43.9\%$ of the Portuguese mentions, on a par with the $44.5\%$ it reaches on English Indian judgments, and only German collapses. What singles out German-LER is as much its schema as its language, since legal-norm and case-law citations make up $62\%$ of its mentions and are long multi-token strings, while our alignment credits a mention only when both character offsets match exactly. Cross-lingual transfer and long-span detection are therefore confounded in that column, and separating them would take a boundary-relaxed alignment, which we leave to future work. Either way the reading is the same, and it localises where domain adaptation would pay off first: a light, in-language adaptation of the \emph{detector} (or a multilingual one) would lift the ceiling far more than a better typing head. Within our frugal, retraining-free budget, OPENER-Sup is therefore the practical default for legal and other applied deployments that can supply even a modest labelled sample, while OPENER-ZS remains a useful no-label fallback only when the target language stays close to the embedder's own (English, general) training distribution.

\textbf{Fusion weight $\beta$.} The fusion weight $\beta$ (fixed at $0.05$, inherited from the general-domain study and never tuned on the legal test sets) balances the label-name centroids against the detector's own zero-shot labels. Sweeping it post-hoc on the released model, a pure inference hyperparameter that leaves the embedder untouched, tells the same story: at $\beta{=}0$ the refined prototypes alone reach $32.4$ average AMI ($30.5$ without the transductive pass), and as $\beta$ grows the head interpolates smoothly up to the GLiNER-L labels ($35.0$), a plateau it reaches by $\beta{\approx}0.2$. The label-free head can therefore do no better end-to-end than the detector it fuses with. The sweep also exposes a component that does not survive the transfer: at $\beta{=}0.05$, dropping the transductive pass would \emph{raise} the average from $33.7$ to $34.8$, because re-estimating prototypes on test mentions helps when those are mostly true entities, as in the general domain, whereas here two thirds of the detected spans are alignment noise and the refined centroids drift towards it. We keep the released configuration rather than tune it on legal test sets, but under a domain and language shift the label-free components are clearly the ones to re-examine first.
