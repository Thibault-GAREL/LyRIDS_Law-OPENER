\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Named Entity Recognition (NER) extracts entities such as persons, organisations and dates from raw text, and is a foundational step in most information extraction pipelines. Standard NER systems are closed-set: trained once on a fixed inventory of types, they can only predict those types. Real-world applications rarely afford such a static inventory, and legal text is an extreme case. Entity inventories are open and shifting (new statutes, case citations, parties, courts, contractual instruments, jurisdictions), documents are long and formulaic, annotation requires expert effort, and extraction errors carry material consequences. Re-annotating and retraining a tagger for every new type or sub-domain is rarely practical.

This is precisely the regime that \emph{open-world} NER targets: recognising and typing entities whose categories were not fixed at training time. Notable open-world systems take different routes to it: span scoring against type names \citep{zaratiana2024gliner}, generative tagging \citep{ding2024gner}, a dedicated entity encoder clustered into dynamically named types \citep{genest2025owner}, and prompted instruction-tuned models \citep{zhou2024universalner}, at far higher inference compute. All are trained and evaluated on general-domain text, and their legal uses adapt them per corpus, schema and language, by fine-tuning a tagger or by prompting a large model anew. Few legal deployments can pay that price for every schema, sub-domain and jurisdiction, so we take the opposite route: one open-world pipeline whose components are never fitted to a legal corpus, measured component by component to locate the bottleneck and show where the next annotation or compute budget should go.

We do this with OPENER, a frugal open-world pipeline we designed for the legal setting from ready-to-use pretrained components: a frozen open-world detector followed by a light contrastively fine-tuned, Matryoshka-truncatable entity embedder. Its embedder is fine-tuned on general-domain entity spans and never on a legal schema, a design choice rather than a shortcut: an open-world space must stay unspecialised, or it collapses into the closed-set taggers we are trying to avoid. It also runs one to two orders of magnitude cheaper in latency and energy than trained-encoder and generative baselines, which legal practice, rarely GPU-rich, needs. We evaluate its two operating points: OPENER-ZS, a label-free head (label-name prototypes, transductive refinement, detector fusion), and OPENER-Sup, a balanced linear probe fit on the legal target labels. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A frugal pipeline for legal NER.} We assemble an open-world pipeline from pretrained components with \emph{no} legal-domain training of any of them, and we test both of its typing heads, the label-free OPENER-ZS and the supervised OPENER-Sup.
\item \textbf{A legal open-world benchmark.} Four legal NER corpora in three languages: English (SEC/EDGAR filings and Indian court judgements), Portuguese and German (Brazilian and federal court decisions). Eight systems are compared end to end under one protocol: both OPENER heads, the standard open-world baselines (GLiNER-S/M/L, GNER-T5 and OWNER), and a 4-bit Qwen2.5-1.5B.
\item \textbf{A three-axis, seed-averaged protocol.} We report quality (AMI), latency and energy jointly rather than accuracy alone, and average every OPENER score over three embedder-retraining seeds.
\item \textbf{An explanation of why the two heads diverge.} OPENER-ZS stays usable on English legal text (up to 49.1 gold AMI) but does not lead the zero-shot field, trailing GLiNER-M/L and, on average, OWNER. OPENER-Sup is barely dented by the domain shift ($61.4$ mean gold AMI, on a par with the $62.3$ the same head reaches in a preliminary general-domain check) and posts the best end-to-end score (35.9), because its balanced probe draws boundaries between closely related legal types that a single label-name match cannot. Both heads are then capped by the English detectors' recall (down to $18.7\%$ on German for GLiNER-L, $10.2\%$ for GLiNER-M), which makes in-language \emph{detection}, not typing, the first place further adaptation should go. Keeping the embedding space unspecialised therefore costs little, which is what motivates the general-domain study of the same pipeline we are now carrying out.
\end{itemize}
