\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Named Entity Recognition (NER) extracts entities such as persons, organisations and dates from raw text, and is a foundational step in most information extraction pipelines. Standard NER systems are closed-set: they are trained once on a fixed inventory of types and can only predict those types. Real-world applications rarely afford such a static inventory, and legal text is an extreme case. Entity inventories are open and shifting (new statutes, case citations, parties, courts, contractual instruments, jurisdictions), documents are long and formulaic, annotation requires expert effort, and extraction errors carry material consequences. Re-annotating and retraining a closed-set tagger for every new type or sub-domain is therefore rarely practical.

This is precisely the regime that \emph{open-world} NER targets: recognising and typing entities whose categories were not fixed at training time. Notable open-world systems take different routes to this goal: GLiNER \citep{zaratiana2024gliner} scores candidate spans against type names supplied as queries, GNER \citep{ding2024gner} recasts NER as text generation, OWNER \citep{genest2025owner} trains a dedicated entity encoder and clusters mentions into dynamically named types, and instruction-tuned language models can be prompted directly, at the cost of far higher inference compute. All are trained and evaluated on general-domain text, and their legal uses adapt them per corpus, schema and language, by fine-tuning a tagger or by prompting a large model anew. Whether an open-world system trained on \emph{general-domain} data can simply be \emph{reused} on legal text, without any legal-domain retraining, is left open.

We study this with OPENER \citep{anon2026opener}, a frugal open-world pipeline assembled from off-the-shelf pretrained components: a frozen open-world detector followed by a contrastively fine-tuned, Matryoshka-truncatable entity embedder. On a general-domain benchmark, it is competitive in accuracy while staying one to two orders of magnitude cheaper in latency and energy than trained-encoder and generative baselines. We take it exactly as released and apply it to legal NER, reusing its entity embedder from the Hugging Face Hub unchanged. We evaluate its two operating points: OPENER-ZS, a label-free head (label-name prototypes, transductive refinement, detector fusion), and OPENER-Sup, a balanced linear probe fit on the legal target labels. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item A \textbf{transfer study} of a frugal, general-domain open-world NER pipeline to the legal domain, with \emph{no} legal-domain retraining of any component.
\item An evaluation on \textbf{four legal NER corpora spanning three languages}: English (SEC/EDGAR filings and Indian court judgments), Portuguese (Brazilian court decisions) and German (federal court decisions). We report three axes at once (quality (AMI), latency and energy) for both typing heads, against the same open-world baselines as the general-domain benchmark (GLiNER, GNER, OWNER, a 4-bit LLM), with every OPENER score averaged over three embedder-retraining seeds.
\item An analysis of \textbf{why the two heads diverge under transfer}. OPENER-ZS remains among the most competitive label-free systems on English legal text (up to 49.1 gold AMI), but it no longer leads every compared zero-shot system as it did in the general domain, falling behind the GLiNER family and, on average, behind OWNER. OPENER-Sup is barely affected by the domain shift (61.4 mean gold AMI, against 62.3 in the general domain) and posts the best end-to-end score (35.9). Its balanced linear probe fits separating hyperplanes fine enough to distinguish closely related legal types, such as lawyers from judges or statutes from case law, even where the shared embedding space places their vectors close together, whereas the zero-shot head's label-name matching cannot sharpen that same boundary without supervision. This makes the supervised head the more practical choice whenever legal, or other applied, deployments can supply labelled examples, and points to in-language \emph{detection}, not typing, as the first place further domain adaptation would pay off.
\end{itemize}
