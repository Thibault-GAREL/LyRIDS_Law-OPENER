\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Named Entity Recognition (NER) extracts entities such as persons, organisations and dates from raw text, and is a foundational step in most information extraction pipelines. Standard NER systems are closed-set: trained once on a fixed inventory of types, they can only predict those types. Real-world applications rarely afford such a static inventory, and legal text is an extreme case. Entity inventories are open and shifting (new statutes, case citations, parties, courts, contractual instruments, jurisdictions), documents are long and formulaic, annotation requires expert effort, and extraction errors carry material consequences. Re-annotating and retraining a tagger for every new type or sub-domain is rarely practical.

This is precisely the regime that \emph{open-world} NER targets: recognising and typing entities whose categories were not fixed at training time. Notable open-world systems take different routes to this goal: GLiNER \citep{zaratiana2024gliner} scores candidate spans against type names supplied as queries, GNER \citep{ding2024gner} recasts NER as text generation, OWNER \citep{genest2025owner} trains a dedicated entity encoder and clusters mentions into dynamically named types, and instruction-tuned language models such as UniversalNER \citep{zhou2024universalner} can be prompted directly, at the cost of far higher inference compute. All are trained and evaluated on general-domain text, and their legal uses adapt them per corpus, schema and language, by fine-tuning a tagger or by prompting a large model anew. Whether an open-world system trained on \emph{general-domain} data can simply be reused on legal text, without any legal-domain retraining, is left open. Answering it serves whoever has to build a specialised NER system: measuring a ready-to-use pipeline component by component locates the bottleneck, and shows where the next annotation or compute budget should go.

We study this with OPENER \citep{anon2026opener}, a frugal and efficient open-world pipeline assembled from ready-to-use pretrained components: a frozen open-world detector followed by a light contrastively fine-tuned, Matryoshka-truncatable entity embedder. On a general-domain benchmark, it is competitive in accuracy while staying one to two orders of magnitude cheaper in latency and energy than trained-encoder and generative baselines. We take it exactly as released and apply it to legal NER, reusing its published entity embedder unchanged. We evaluate its two operating points: OPENER-ZS, a label-free head (label-name prototypes, transductive refinement, detector fusion), and OPENER-Sup, a balanced linear probe fit on the legal target labels. Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A transfer test.} We reuse a general-domain open-world pipeline on legal text with \emph{no} legal-domain retraining of any component, and we test both of its typing heads, the label-free OPENER-ZS and the supervised OPENER-Sup.
\item \textbf{A legal open-world benchmark.} Four legal NER corpora in three languages: English (SEC/EDGAR filings and Indian court judgements), Portuguese and German (Brazilian and federal court decisions). Eight systems are compared end to end under one protocol: both OPENER heads, the general-domain benchmark's own baselines (GLiNER-S/M/L, GNER-T5 and OWNER), and a 4-bit Qwen2.5-1.5B.
\item \textbf{A three-axis, seed-averaged protocol.} We report quality (AMI), latency and energy jointly rather than accuracy alone, and average every OPENER score over three embedder-retraining seeds.
\item \textbf{An explanation of why the two heads diverge.} OPENER-ZS stays usable on English legal text (up to 49.1 gold AMI) but no longer leads as it did in the general domain, trailing GLiNER-M/L and, on average, OWNER. OPENER-Sup is barely affected (61.4 mean gold AMI, against 62.3 in the general domain) and posts the best end-to-end score (35.9), because its balanced probe draws boundaries between closely related legal types that a single label-name match cannot. Both heads are then capped by the English detectors' recall (down to $18.7\%$ on German for GLiNER-L, $10.2\%$ for GLiNER-M), which makes in-language \emph{detection}, not typing, the first place further adaptation should go.
\end{itemize}
